For the same twisted-star gate set, **the five-gate walk has conductance exactly zero for every even \(m>4r\)** once \(N=3r+1\) and \(r\ge 11\). This holds even if we allow **every** product-preserving rewrite of five consecutive gates. At each of those lengths, the full identity fiber contains an isolated circuit.

There is also a positive general-length theorem. On the **same gate set and the same full identity fiber**, windows of width \(O(N\log N)\) suffice for inverse-polynomial conductance at every nonempty length. The distinction is how many consecutive gates one move can change.

## The state space and the obstruction

Recall the alphabet. Let \(N=3r+1\), with \(3\nmid r\), and set
\[
t_a=(1\,a),\qquad
\rho=(2\,3\,4)(5\,6\,7)\cdots(N-2\,\,N-1\,\,N),\qquad
g_a=t_a\rho .
\]
There are \(N-1\) gates. The state space \(\Omega_m\) consists of **all** ordered length-\(m\) words in these gates whose product is the identity. A width-\(k\) move replaces at most \(k\) consecutive gates by the same number of gates with the same product.

**Zero-conductance theorem.** Let \(k\ge2\) and \(r\ge2k+1\). Every width-\(k\) walk on \(\Omega_m\) with uniform stationary distribution has \(\Phi=0\) in either of these ranges:

- Every even \(m>4r\).
- Every multiple of six \(m\ge4k-2\), including those below \(4r\).

At the particular length \(m=2N-2\), the obstruction is stronger. **Even width \(\lfloor N/2\rfloor\) leaves an isolated circuit.**

The reason is visible directly in a circuit. Given its gate labels \(a_1,\ldots,a_m\), define \(u_j=\rho^{j-1}(a_j)\). Then
\[
g_{a_1}\cdots g_{a_m}
=t_{u_1}\cdots t_{u_m}\rho^m.
\]
This is a position-by-position bijection, so it preserves the width of every rewrite. In these coordinates, there is an exact test:

> A circuit is isolated under all width-\(k\) rewrites if and only if every window of at most \(k\) normalized labels has pairwise distinct labels.

Indeed, distinct labels \(i_1,\ldots,i_d\) produce the cycle \((1\,i_d\,\ldots\,i_1)\). Any length-\(d\) spelling must use each of those \(d\) leaves, and the cycle determines their order. Conversely, two closest equal labels in a window enclose either a square, which can be refreshed, or a leaf cycle, which can be respelled starting at a different leaf.

For a concrete example, take **\(N=13\) and \(m=24\)**. Concatenate these two rows of normalized labels:
\[
\begin{aligned}
&(2,12,11,10,9,8,7,6,5,4,3,2),\\
&(7,8,9,10,11,12,2,3,4,5,6,7).
\end{aligned}
\]
The two rows spell inverse cycles, and \(\rho^{24}=e\), so this is an identity circuit. Check any six consecutive labels, including across the join. They are all distinct. **There is no possible first move**, even though the same fiber also contains, for example, the circuit with all normalized labels equal to \(2\).

## Why this happens at general lengths

Write \(E(P;x)\) for the star spelling of a leaf cycle \(P=(x\,x_1\,\ldots\,x_{d-1})\) that starts at \(x\):
\[
E(P;x)=t_x t_{x_{d-1}}\cdots t_{x_1}t_x.
\]
Its only repeated label is \(x\), at distance \(d\). The following constructions give a frozen circuit at **each** length in the theorem.

**When \(6\mid m\)**, the normalized target is the identity. Take a cycle \(P\) on \(2k-2\) leaves. Spell \(P\) and \(P^{-1}\) starting at leaves halfway apart around the cycle. The resulting word has length \(4k-2\), and every \(k\)-window has distinct labels. To reach any larger even length, surround the spelling of \(P\) by \(Y\) and \(Y^{-1}\), where \(Y\) cycles through \(k\) unused leaves. Every letter of \(Y\) commutes with the permutation \(P\), and the short windows still have distinct labels. Taking \(P\) on \(N-2\) leaves instead gives the stronger example at \(m=2N-2\).

**When \(3\nmid m\)**, the normalized target \(\rho^{-m}\) moves every leaf. Write it as \(\prod_{i=1}^r(a_i\,b_i\,c_i)\). The useful factorization is
\[
\begin{aligned}
P&=(a_1\,b_1\,a_2\,b_2\,\cdots\,a_r\,b_r),\\
Q&=(b_r\,c_r\,b_{r-1}\,c_{r-1}\,\cdots\,b_1\,c_1).
\end{aligned}
\]
Direct multiplication gives \(PQ=\rho^{-m}\). For any even \(m>4r\), take
\[
Y\,E(P;a_1)\,Y^{-1}\,E(Q;b_{k+1}),
\qquad |Y|=\frac{m-4r-2}{2},
\]
where \(Y\) cycles through \(c_1,\ldots,c_k\). These letters commute with \(P\). At the join, the last \(k-1\) labels of \(E(P;a_1)\) belong to triples with indices at most \(k\), while the first \(k-1\) labels of \(E(Q;b_{k+1})\) belong to triples with indices greater than \(k\). Thus every short window is rigid. In this case the frozen circuit uses **every leaf**, so requiring full support does not remove the obstruction.

## A positive conductance theorem for every length

Let \(B=\lceil C N\log(N+1)\rceil\) for a sufficiently large absolute constant \(C\), and let \(q=\max\{1,\lfloor m/B\rfloor\}\). The following walk on \(\Omega_m\) has
\[
\boxed{\displaystyle \Phi\ge \frac{c}{q}}
\]
for an absolute constant \(c>0\).

1. Divide the word into \(q\) consecutive blocks. The first \(q-1\) have length \(B\), and the last takes the remainder.
2. With probability \(1/2\), stay put. Otherwise choose a uniformly random neighboring pair of blocks and resample their union uniformly among words of the same length and product. If \(q=1\), resample the single block.

Each move changes at most \(\min(m,3B)\) consecutive gates. Verification consists of comparing the two block products and checking that the exterior agrees.

The conductance bound comes from looking at the partial products at block boundaries. After \(B\) random star transpositions, each permutation of the required parity has probability within 1% of \(2/N!\). This follows from the \(L^2\) estimate for the star walk by pairing the positive and negative eigenvalues of the walk without holding and doubling the time. Consequently, changing a neighboring boundary changes the conditional distribution at a boundary by less than \(0.1\) in total variation. Updating a disagreeing boundary erases that disagreement, while only its two neighbors can create another. This gives contraction at rate \(1-\Omega(1/q)\). Once the boundaries agree, updating a pair couples its interiors as well. The full walk therefore has spectral gap \(\Omega(1/q)\), giving the displayed conductance bound. genui{"citation":{"ref":"turn301807965view0"}}

The block update is also **exactly sampleable in expected polynomial time**. The Goulden–Jackson formula counts star factorizations of arbitrary length on a specified used support. Summing over the number of optional fixed leaves used gives the number of completions after each possible next gate. These counts require only polynomial-degree univariate coefficient computations. They let us sample a uniform replacement one gate at a time. genui{"citation":{"refs":["turn324153147view0","turn324153147view1"]}}

If you also want **polynomially many possible moves per circuit**, there is an explicit variant. Order the \(M\) valid block replacements lexicographically and change the current rank by \(\pm2^j\bmod M\), choosing \(j\) from \(O(B\log N)\) possibilities. The same counts compute ranks and their inverses. For each nonzero cyclic Fourier frequency, at least one shift puts its phase between a quarter-turn and a half-turn, up to sign. The resulting block walk has gap \(\Omega(1/(B\log N))\), giving full-fiber conductance \(\Omega(1/(qB\log N))\). This still gives polynomial mixing for every \(m=\mathrm{poly}(N)\).

For your original locality requirement, the remaining gap is fairly narrow. At **every even \(m>4r\)**, some circuit forces the window width to be **\(\Omega(N)\)**. Width **\(O(N\log N)\)** suffices. At linear \(m\) the positive walk can refresh the whole circuit, whereas for \(m\gg N\log N\) it changes a vanishing fraction of the circuit per step. Whether width \(O(N)\) suffices throughout that long-word regime is the question these bounds leave open.
